## 2. Answers

### Q1. Has anyone decomposed verifier AUROC into instance difficulty vs within-instance discrimination, or proposed within-instance AUROC as the metric for best-of-k verifiers?

Pieces of it exist; the decomposition itself was not found.

What exists:
- **Pairwise accuracy within a prompt** is the standard RM metric (RewardBench-style), and N16 studies how the way accuracy is measured changes its predictiveness of best-of-n regret, including that more responses per prompt helps. It does not decompose a pooled score.
- **Selection on the rerankable (mixed-outcome) subset.** N1 evaluates Best@8 only on SWE-bench instances "where at least one run succeeds and at least one fails" because "only these instances admit improvements over random selection". This is within-instance evaluation of a SWE critic, published March 2026, so "evaluate verifiers within the issue" is not ours to claim. N1 does not report within-instance AUROC, a difficulty-prior baseline, or the pooled-vs-within gap.
- **Per-prompt AUROC as a diagnostic.** N18 (App. B.1) computes AUROC separately for each prompt's rollout group and reports the distribution (mean 0.79, median 0.92). That is a within-prompt AUROC, used to check an RM during RL, not proposed as an evaluation metric for verifiers.
- **Per-problem ranking metrics.** N17 normalises reward scores within each problem and averages Top-1/Bottom-1/Spearman across problems for code verifiers (HumanEval/MBPP level, not agent runs).
- **Within-instance training signal.** N9 (FailFast, §3.1) builds Bradley–Terry pairs only from mixed instances, matched by progress, explicitly "to control for both instance difficulty and trajectory progress, isolating an instance-invariant ranking signal". That is the training-side analogue of our idea, for prefix-abort rather than final-patch verification.
- **Baseline-subtracted scores.** Advantage-style verifiers (N26) and group-normalised rewards (N18's stratified normalisation; GRPO-style group baselines in general) remove the per-prompt mean, which is the same confound handled in RL rather than evaluation.
- **Pooled AUROC is what SWE verifier papers report.** N12 (SWE-RM §4.1): "AUC score across all 32×500 trajectories". N11 (SWE-Gym) reports only Best@k. Neither stratifies by instance difficulty.

What was not found: (a) a decomposition of a verifier's pooled AUROC into an instance-prior component and a within-instance component; (b) a leave-one-out instance pass-rate as the baseline a verifier must beat (our 0.958); (c) a demonstration that a verifier can have high pooled AUROC and chance-level within-instance AUROC (our TF-IDF 0.96 vs 0.50) on agent trajectories. Searches for "within-problem", "per-question AUROC", "conditional discrimination", "difficulty confound", "instance-level leakage" and "group-aware evaluation" in the verifier/RM literature returned nothing matching. IRT (N30, N31) separates difficulty and ability for rubric criteria and graders, not for run-level verifiers. Our evaluation-protocol contribution is therefore original in its specific form, but it must be written as "decomposition and prior baseline" and cite N1, N9, N16, N17, N18 as the pieces that already exist.

### Q2. Has anyone measured train/test leakage across runs of the same task for learned trajectory verifiers?

No measurement was found for SWE or agent-outcome verifiers. The field's practice is to avoid the leak by construction, without quantifying it:
- Grouped-by-task splits: N9 (FailFast, App. D), N10 (EarlyEval, plus leave-one-agent-out), N13 (Doomed, "Grouped splits avoid task leakage but remain in-distribution", no numbers), N14 (trajectory-grouped).
- Benchmark-disjoint: SWE-Gym (N11) trains on 11 repositories "separate from those used in SWE-Bench"; SWE-RM (N12) trains on SWE-Gym/SWE-rebench/SWE-smith/R2E-Gym and evaluates on SWE-bench Verified; N2 trains the critic on "the same problem instances" as the generator and evaluates on SWE-bench Verified. These are task- and mostly repository-disjoint, but none reports what a random-run split would have given.
- The closest measured number is N15, in a different task: for attack detection on agent tool-call traces, "naive random-split evaluation inflates AUROC by up to 26 percentage points relative to task-disjoint splits". N3 gives the mechanism for our setting: execution-free verifiers "often rely on stylistic features".
- N1 reports a different shift: benchmark-trained critics reach "AUC 0.45–0.48" on production data.

So "random-run splits inflate trajectory-verifier AUROC (0.96 vs 0.70 by repository) and the within-issue AUROC exposes it (0.50)" is, as far as found, the first measurement for SWE trajectories, and the three-level ladder (run / issue / repository) is new. Correct the `refs.md` note: SWE-Gym does split by repository by design, so the claim must be "no one has measured the leak", not "no one splits by repository".

### Q3. Self-learning from deployment outcomes with a statistical promotion gate for a small verifier: what is closest?

Closest pieces, none combining all four of (real outcomes, small verifier, recalibrate + retrain, statistical gate):
- N1: a 4B critic learned from sparse real-world outcome proxies (PR merge, code survival) plus rubric features. It is trained once offline on historical data; no continual loop, no acceptance gate.
- SEAL (arXiv:2607.24300, refs.md): an external harness-side acceptance gate comparing candidate to incumbent, but for self-authored tests in self-improving agents, not for real outcomes.
- ASG-SI (arXiv:2512.23760, refs.md): verifier-gated promotion of skills.
- Verification Horizon (arXiv:2606.26300, refs.md): argues verification must co-evolve with the policy; no mechanism for a small verifier.
- N8 (ExecuCritic): critic and coder updated jointly on execution rollouts; keeping the critic calibrated as the coder drifts is left as future work.
- N34: position paper that deployed systems receiving an evaluative signal are continual-RL problems.
- N35 and the "negative flips" literature: regression-free model updates, which is what a promotion gate protects against, but as a training objective, not a statistical gate.
- Champion/challenger and shadow deployment with bootstrap CIs are standard MLOps practice (grey literature only; no arXiv paper found). Do not claim the gate idea; claim the design.

How ours differs: (1) outcomes are hidden-test results arriving later, not merge proxies; (2) the unit of the bootstrap is the repository, not the run, because runs of one issue and issues of one repository are correlated (S1 is the classical statement that ignoring within-cluster correlation makes intervals too narrow); (3) the gate is on held-out log loss, which also catches calibration regressions that AUROC-based gates miss; (4) the challenger is a LoRA plus Platt on a 4B-class model, cheap enough to retrain on a laptop. Items (2) and (3) are the original parts; (1) and (4) are engineering.

### Q4. Is a "calibrated multi-question rubric in one forward pass" new for verifying agent runs?

Mostly not, as a mechanism:
- N1 already does the closest thing for agent runs: one Qwen3-4B pass with a multi-task head predicting 24 rubric features and success probability.
- N22 (ArmoRM) predicts many interpretable objectives in one pass; N20 (YOJO) scores many responses in one pass; N23 (IPPD) answers many questions over a shared context in one pass with position-ID and mask tricks, the same idea as our block-causal branches, for speed.
- N24, N25 and Agentic Rubrics (arXiv:2601.04171) score rubrics generatively; being discriminative instead is a cost argument, not a novelty.

What remains ours: the questions are natural-language typed questions answered through a pointer head, so a new rubric item needs no new head or retraining, each answer is a calibrated probability, and the branches are isolated by the mask so answers cannot leak into each other. That is a useful property, not a headline. Lead with it only as the enabler of the two-question verifier in P1.

## 3. Ranked proposals (originality × feasibility, five weeks, M1 Max, $0–15)

### P1. Difficulty-decomposed verification: two questions in one pass (rank 1)
Claim: a verifier should output P(issue is solvable by this agent) and P(this run solved it | issue), and should be judged on the second. Implementation: add a second typed question to the same pass, "Would a typical run of this agent resolve this issue?", trained on the leave-one-out issue pass rate from the other runs of the issue; keep "Did this run resolve the issue?" and train it either (a) with within-issue pairwise/BT targets on mixed issues (N9's trick, moved from prefix-abort to final-patch verification) or (b) as a residual on top of the difficulty question. Derived score for best-of-k: logit P(run) − logit P(difficulty).
Experiment: on held-out repositories report (i) pooled AUROC decomposed into prior-explained and within-issue parts, (ii) within-issue AUROC, (iii) best-of-8 vs random and vs LOO-prior on mixed-outcome issues, (iv) calibration of the difficulty question against empirical pass rates (reliability diagram), (v) ablation: single question vs two questions vs pairwise-trained. Baselines: heuristics, TF-IDF, zero-shot D1A, current trained D1A.
Prior art to cite: N1 (mixed-outcome evaluation), N9 (instance-invariant pairs), N26 and N18 (baseline subtraction), N30/N31 (IRT difficulty/ability), N32/N33 (difficulty prediction from the prompt). None combines difficulty estimation and run verification in one verifier evaluated within-instance on SWE runs.
Feasibility: data and LoRA pipeline exist; one adapter per variant, a few hours each on MLX. The binding constraint is statistical: 23 mixed issues is thin. Lift the four-runs-per-issue cap for test repositories (the dataset has ~22 runs per issue) to get every mixed issue with all its runs, and report CIs by issue-clustered bootstrap. Risk: within-issue gains are small (0.617 → 0.661 so far); the two-question design may not move them. The decomposition result stands even if the model does not improve.

### P2. The within-issue correlation study: leakage ladder and clustered promotion gates (rank 2)
Claim: runs of one issue are not exchangeable; ignoring this inflates evaluation (random-run splits) and makes i.i.d. promotion gates anti-conservative.
Experiment A (leakage ladder): for TF-IDF, heuristics and D1A, AUROC under run / issue / repository splits, each paired with within-issue AUROC and the LOO-prior AUROC, on nebius/SWE-agent-trajectories and, if time allows, nebius/SWE-rebench-openhands-trajectories as a second agent (cross-agent transfer of the within-issue signal; N10 does leave-one-agent-out for early prediction, not for final verification).
Experiment B (gates): simulate the feedback loop from the dataset (stream outcomes by repository), train a deliberately leaked challenger (random-run split) and an honest one, and compare three gates on false-promotion rate and held-out log loss: i.i.d. bootstrap over runs, clustered over issues, clustered over repositories. Expected result: the run-level gate promotes the leaked challenger; the repository-level gate does not.
Prior art: N15 (26pp inflation in a different task), N3 (stylistic shortcuts), N9/N10/N13 (grouped splits by principle), S1 (clustered AUC variance), N35 (regression-free updates), SEAL. Nobody has measured either effect for trajectory verifiers.
Feasibility: `leakage.py` and `self_improve.py` already do most of A and B; cost is CPU time. Lowest risk of the three; the result is a negative/protocol finding, which reviewers value less than a method, hence rank 2. P1 and P2 fit in one paper: P2 motivates, P1 is the method.

### P3. Set-conditioned verification: "which of these k runs resolves the issue?" as one choice question (rank 3)
Claim: scoring the k runs of an issue jointly (one block-causal pass, one choice question over k branches) beats scoring them independently for best-of-k, at the cost of one pass.
Prior art is dense: N19 (set-conditioned verifier, math), N20 (multi-response in one pass), N4 (group-wise patch verification for SWE), N21 (pairwise tournaments). The novelty would be only "in a 4B-class model, one pass, patches plus trajectory tails". Feasible (D1A already answers choice questions), but context length limits k when trajectories are included; patch-only branches are realistic. Run it only if P1 and P2 are done, as an extra row in the best-of-k table.
